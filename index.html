The Digital Divide in New York State's Animal Welfare Marketing

An Analysis of Instagram Engagement and Posting Behaviors Among NYS Animal Shelters

By  Charlotte Zhao

Social media is often presented as a relatively accessible way for nonprofit organizations to reach an audience, regardless of their size or budget. I tested that assumption by collecting and analyzing public Instagram data from 570 animal welfare organizations across New York State. The results show that posting frequency differs most sharply among the smallest accounts, raising a more practical question: how much does an organization's capacity to produce content shape its ability to stay visible online?

When follower counts are compared with posting intervals, the pattern is not a steady increase in posting frequency as audiences grow. The biggest difference appears among the smallest accounts: organizations below roughly 500 followers post, on median, once every 11 days, more than twice the overall median of 5.13 days. Above that point, posting intervals vary without a consistent relationship to follower count. An account with roughly 1,300 followers and one with roughly 30,000 followers can have similar posting intervals, while the Pearson correlation across the full sample is -0.07.

The median posting interval is 5.1 days, while the mean is 18.2 days. More than a quarter of accounts in the dataset go three weeks or longer between posts.

The pattern holds up geographically, too. Among the 135 accounts with a confirmed New York business address, upstate shelters carry roughly a third of the median follower count of NYC metro shelters, yet post more frequently: a median of 3.08 days between posts, compared with 4.04 days downstate.

Graceland Meadows Animal Rescue and Sanctuary offers a look at what the bottom tier actually looks like up close. It is a registered 501(c)(3) in upstate New York that takes in senior, injured, and surrendered farm animals. Its posting frequency is roughly once every six weeks, and its account has 102 followers. Its engagement rate, at 5.96%, is nearly five times the industry median of 1.25%.

The implications of the gap extend beyond follower counts because social media is one of the public-facing channels through which shelters present their work to potential adopters, donors, volunteers, and supporters. The data cannot show whether a larger Instagram audience translates into more donations or adoptions, but it does show that organizations enter that public-facing space with very different levels of reach and posting activity. For organizations with limited capacity to produce content, maintaining a consistent presence may require resources that are difficult to sustain alongside their other work. If maintaining a digital presence requires organizational capacity that smaller shelters may have less of, then a platform that is free to use does not necessarily offer equal opportunities to build an audience. For New York's mid-size and rural shelters, the more immediate question may be how much time and organizational capacity should be devoted to maintaining an Instagram presence when posting frequency and audience size do not move together consistently across the sector.

Methodology & Data Governance

The primary dataset was constructed by scraping public Instagram profile metrics from 570 animal rescue and shelter accounts across New York State in June and July of 2026. Accounts that were private, inactive, incompletely scraped, or confirmed to be non-shelter or non-New York entities were audited and excluded using Python and Pandas. The final analytical sample comprises 394 organizations, after removing accounts with no posts after January 2025 and accounts confirmed to be non-shelter or non-New York entities. A geographic breakdown, NYC metro versus upstate, was possible for a further subset of 135 accounts with confirmed New York business addresses.

Limitations

This baseline analysis relies purely on public-facing digital output. It cannot fully quantify the invisible labor of volunteer-run organizations, and the scraper's structural limit of 12 captured posts per account means posting frequency is measured over a short recent window, not a full account history. During validation, at least one account showed a captured latest post date years out of step with its actual, visibly recent activity, suggesting some scraping gaps that could not be fully distinguished from genuinely inactive accounts.
